## The Problem

When you move to a new neighborhood — or want to rediscover your own — there's no good tool. Google Maps is global and impersonal. Yelp drowns in reviews. Instagram is algorithmic and ad-saturated. None of them capture what locals actually know.

The places worth knowing are not the ones with 4.2 stars. They're the ones your neighbor tells you about on the first morning you meet.

<Callout type="insight">
The gap is not a lack of information about places — it's a lack of trusted, specific, local knowledge. A recommendation from someone who lives three streets away is worth more than a thousand anonymous reviews.
</Callout>

## Research

I interviewed 15 people who had recently moved to a new area or returned from time abroad. Key findings:

- Every person described relying on personal networks (neighbors, colleagues, WhatsApp groups) for local discovery — not apps
- When they did use apps, they described "filtering fatigue" — too many results with too little signal
- The most cherished local knowledge was highly specific: "the bakery only has croissants until 9am"
- 11 of 15 said they would share their own local knowledge if the format felt casual and low-effort

The opportunity: an app that captures the texture and specificity of local knowledge — without the overhead of writing a review.

## Design Approach

**Vibe over category.** Instead of browsing by "Restaurants" or "Parks," users browse by vibe: quiet, lively, good for kids, date spot, early morning, work-friendly. Places can have multiple vibes. This is how people actually think about where they want to go.

**Place cards, not reviews.** A Scout entry is not a review — it's a local telling you one specific thing about a place. Short, specific, human. "The corner table by the window is worth waiting for." That's the whole post.

**Neighborhood maps as identity.** Your personal map — the places you've added — becomes a record of your neighborhood life. Shareable with friends moving to the area.

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Explore" title="The map view">
    A warm, illustrated map with custom pin styles per vibe type. Clusters resolve as you zoom. Your own pins have a different visual treatment from community pins.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Discover" title="Vibe browsing">
    A horizontal scroll of vibe tags above the map. Tap "Quiet" and the map filters to quiet spots only — a library corner, a hidden garden, a cafe with a no-laptop policy.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Contribute" title="Adding a place">
    A 3-step flow: tap the map, name and categorize, add your one-sentence local tip. Under 60 seconds, designed to feel like texting a friend.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Share" title="Your neighborhood map">
    A shareable version of your personal map — useful for friends visiting or neighbors asking where to eat. It's a living, personal guide to your neighborhood.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

## Key Design Decisions

**No ratings or scores.** Every score becomes a race to the top, homogenizing recommendations. Scout has no ratings — only presence and the quality of the specific tip.

**Contributor identity.** You see who added a place — their neighborhood, how long they've lived there. This context makes a recommendation meaningful.

**Illustration over satellite maps.** A custom illustrated map style (warm terracotta and sand tones) makes Scout feel like a handdrawn neighborhood guide, not a logistics tool.

## Visual Direction

Terracotta, sand, and off-white. Photography-forward place cards. Custom illustrated map pins in warm ink. The overall aesthetic should feel like a handwritten recommendation — considered, specific, local.

## Outcome

This is a speculative project demonstrating community product design: map-based UX, user-generated content flows, place card design, vibe-based taxonomy, onboarding for location permissions, and the challenge of designing for contribution — making adding content feel low-effort and natural.
