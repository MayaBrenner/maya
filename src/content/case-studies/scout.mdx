## The Problem

When you move to a new neighborhood — or want to rediscover your own — there's no good tool. Google Maps is global and impersonal. Yelp drowns in review volume. Instagram is algorithmic and saturated. None of them capture what locals actually know.

The places worth knowing aren't the ones with 4.2 stars. They're the ones your neighbor tells you about on the first morning you meet.

<Callout type="insight">
The gap isn't a lack of information about places — it's a lack of trusted, specific, local knowledge. A recommendation from someone who lives three streets away and has lived there for two years is worth more than a thousand anonymous reviews. Scout is designed for that recommendation.
</Callout>

## Research

I interviewed 15 people who had recently moved to a new area or returned from time abroad. I was specifically interested in how they built local knowledge — not what apps they used.

**What people actually said:**

- *"The bakery only has croissants until 9am"* — hyper-specific, time-bound, impossible to find in a review
- *"The park at the end of the street is amazing at golden hour — no one knows about it"* — local and personal
- *"I always ask my neighbor. She's lived here 12 years."* — trust based on context, not rating count

## Design Decisions

### Vibe over category

Most discovery apps organize by category (Restaurants · Parks · Cafes). Scout organizes by vibe: quiet, lively, good for kids, date spot, early morning, work-friendly.

This is how people actually think about where to go. "I want somewhere quiet to read" is a vibe. It maps to a library corner, a hidden garden, a cafe with a no-laptop policy — not a single category.

### Place cards, not reviews

A Scout entry is not a review. It's a local telling you one specific thing about a place. Short. Specific. Human. The format is closer to a text message than a Yelp review.

*"The corner table by the window gets afternoon light from 2–4pm. Worth asking for it."*

That's a Scout card. No star rating. No "Overall experience" field.

### No ratings or scores

Every rating system eventually produces the same thing: a race to the top that homogenizes recommendations toward what's safe and popular. Scout has no ratings.

Presence is the signal. If someone added a place, they think it's worth knowing about.

### Contributor identity matters

You see who added a place — their neighborhood and how long they've lived there. *"Added by someone who's lived in Florentin for 6 years"* makes that recommendation meaningful in a way an anonymous 4-star rating never is.

## Core Flow

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Explore" title="The map — your neighborhood, visualized">
    A warm illustrated map with custom pin styles per vibe. Clusters resolve as you zoom. Your pins have a distinct visual treatment from community pins — you always know what's yours.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Discover" title="Vibe browsing — filter to what you want">
    Horizontal scroll of vibe tags above the map. Tap 'Quiet' and the map filters immediately — only quiet spots visible. No search field needed for the common case.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Contribute" title="Adding a place — under 60 seconds">
    Three steps: tap the map, name and categorize, write your one-sentence local tip. The format enforces brevity. If it takes more than 60 seconds, the form is too complex.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Share" title="Your personal map — a living local guide">
    All your added places become a shareable map. Useful for friends moving to the area or neighbors asking where to eat. It's a record of your neighborhood life.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

**Adding a place.** The form enforces the format: name, category, one tip. The tip field has a 120-character limit. Longer than that and it stops being a tip and becomes a review.

The 60-second target shapes every UI decision: no optional fields, no tagging system, no photo upload required. The friction of adding a place is the primary risk to contribution volume.

## Outcome

Scout is a speculative project demonstrating:

- **Community product design** — designing for contribution (the hardest problem in community apps) by reducing friction below the threshold of resistance
- **Map-based UX** — custom pin system, vibe-based taxonomy, cluster behavior, location permission onboarding
- **Trust through identity** — using contributor context instead of rating systems to signal recommendation quality
- **Content format design** — the one-sentence tip is a product decision, not a UI constraint. Enforcing the format is what makes the content useful.
