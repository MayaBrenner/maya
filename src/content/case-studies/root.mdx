## The Problem

You're at the supermarket looking at six varieties of potato, and your recipe says "potatoes." You grab the closest one. Dinner is gluey.

You didn't fail at cooking. You failed at a decision you didn't know you were making.

The difference between a floury King Edward and a waxy Yukon Gold is the difference between a silky purée and a gummy one. That knowledge lives with chefs and grandmothers. It's almost never at the moment of decision — standing under fluorescent lights, basket in hand, seven minutes before your store closes.

<Callout type="insight">
The pivot that defined this project: instead of building a produce app for every vegetable in the store, go deep on one. A single ingredient, treated with Michelin-level seriousness. The potato sommelier — not a produce database.
</Callout>

## Research

I wanted to understand not what people knew about potatoes, but where their confidence broke down — and whether the problem was knowledge or access to it.

I interviewed 12 home cooks, focusing on two groups: cooks who plan ahead (they have a dish, they're at the store to get ingredients) and cooks who buy on impulse (they're at a market stall, something looks good, they figure out the dish later). I expected these to be edge cases. They turned out to describe almost everyone — just at different moments.

## Design Questions

Three questions came out of research that framed everything that followed:

**How might we deliver the right answer at the exact moment of decision** — in the store, potato in hand — without requiring the cook to know what they don't know?

**How might we make AI feel like expertise rather than search** — so the recommendation feels earned and opinionated, not retrieved and neutral?

**How might we turn a failed dish into learning rather than frustration** — so the cook builds knowledge over time instead of repeating the same mistake?

## Design Principles

Before opening Figma, I set three rules for how Root had to behave.

**One ingredient. Deep not wide.** A produce app that covers every vegetable is a search engine. Root covers potatoes only — which is what makes the Chef feel expert. Breadth and credibility are in tension. I chose credibility.

**The Chef has opinions.** Most AI is personality-free to avoid offending anyone. The Chef is the opposite: terse, specific, always in italics, always gold. If the recommendation doesn't have a point of view, it's not advice — it's a lookup.

**Mistakes teach you something.** The standard app flow ends at purchase. Root's loop continues after cooking — because the feedback from a gluey mash is more valuable than the recommendation that caused it. The chef learns from you.

## Architecture: Two Paths, One Chef

The app is built around one AI persona, but users arrive in two fundamentally different states. Designing for both without building two separate apps was the core structural challenge.

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Path 01" title="I have a dish — tell me which potato">
    The cook knows what they're making. They open Root, tell the Chef — by voice or text — and get a recommendation in under 30 seconds. Variety, reasoning, and a three-rule in-store checklist.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Path 02" title="I have a potato — tell me what to cook">
    The cook is standing at a market stall, potato in hand, no plan. They point their camera at it. The AI identifies the variety in 0.6 seconds and returns a ranked list of what it's best for — and what to avoid. The inverse of Path 01.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Debrief" title="The feedback loop — the chef learns from you">
    After cooking, one question: how was it? Silky, gluey, or grainy? The answer trains the Chef's model of how this cook handles this variety. Honest feedback is what makes the AI personal over time.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="The Cellar" title="Mastery tracking — six varieties to learn">
    Every dish cooked with Root builds the Cellar — a record of which varieties the cook has mastered and which they're still learning. The real win state isn't a streak. It's the day you stop needing to ask.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

## Key Screens

**The home screen** functions as a menu cover. Two entry points: The Chef (dish-first, dark card) and At the market? (scan-first, lighter surface). The Cellar sits below — your four most-used varieties, at a glance. The visual hierarchy communicates the two paths before a word is read.

**The Chef conversation** uses an ink-on-paper aesthetic throughout. Gold is reserved exclusively for the Chef's responses — every other element is black or cream. The color rule is doing semantic work: you always know when the AI is speaking, even before you read the words.

**The pairing screen** shows three varieties ranked by match, each with a one-sentence plain-language reason. No star ratings. The reason is the recommendation. *"High starch content means it holds its shape under mashing without turning gluey."* That's a chef's answer, not a database row.

**The variety detail** is structured like a tasting menu, not a wiki article. Botanical name in label type. Italic serif headline. Three rows: texture, best for, avoid for. A chef's pull quote. The gold ring around the potato illustration signals "specimen worth studying" — not stock art.

The format is the opinion. A wiki article is neutral by design. This isn't — it tells you exactly what to do and what to avoid, in the voice of someone who's cooked it a thousand times.

The three-row structure (texture / best for / avoid for) came from noticing that most food apps bury the avoid information. That's the most useful part. If you know that a waxy potato falls apart in a stew, you'll remember it. Knowing what it's good for is helpful. Knowing what ruins it is unforgettable.

The scan result uses the same wine-label format as the variety detail: variety name, chef verdict, ranked dish list, one explicit avoid.

## Iterations

**The scanner wasn't in the original design.** Path 01 (dish-first) was the whole concept. The scanner came from research: 4 of 12 interviewees described the inverse situation — standing at a market stall with a potato they'd bought on impulse, no dish in mind. The original design had no answer for them. Adding Path 02 doubled the entry surface without adding a second AI or a second database.

**The debrief question started as three steps.** The original post-cooking feedback asked: what did you make, how did it turn out, would you use this variety again? Three questions. In prototype testing, people answered the first one and stopped. The final version is one question — silky, gluey, or grainy — which is both faster and more useful. Texture is what varies. That's the signal worth capturing.

**The Cellar was added late.** Early versions ended at the debrief. There was no record, no accumulation. Users had no reason to return unless they were cooking right now. The Cellar gave the app a long-term shape — something that builds over weeks, not just one session. The win state shifted from "got a recommendation" to "I know this variety well enough not to need Root anymore." That inversion — designing toward your own obsolescence — is what made the retention mechanic feel honest.

The visual language is a deliberate argument: potatoes are as serious as wine. Ink on cream paper. Gold used sparingly. Italic serif for the chef's voice. Montserrat all-caps for metadata. No color photography — potato SVG illustrations instead, drawn in the style of a field guide.

This isn't decoration. It's a claim that this knowledge is worth treating with care.

## Takeaways

The thing I kept returning to throughout this project: the problem was never information. There is infinite information about potatoes online. The problem was trust and timing — someone who could tell you the right thing at the exact moment you needed it.

Designing the Chef's persona was the hardest design decision in the product. Not the architecture, not the visual system — the voice. Getting the Chef to feel opinionated without feeling arrogant, expert without being condescending, required more iteration than any screen. The italic serif and the gold color are doing half the work. The other half is in the exact wording of every response.

The fork-and-merge architecture — two entry paths converging at the same screens — is the structural decision I'm most satisfied with. It would have been easy to build two separate flows. The constraint of one Chef, one Cellar forced a more honest product: you're not choosing a mode, you're just meeting the app where you are.

And narrowing to potatoes only was right. It felt like a limitation at the start. By the end it felt like the whole point. You can't know everything about every vegetable. But you can know everything about one.
