## The Problem

You're standing in the produce aisle holding an avocado. You don't know if it's ripe enough for tonight, or two days away. You put it back. You pick up another one. You're guessing.

This happens to almost everyone. The knowledge exists — farmers, chefs, and grandmothers know exactly what to look for. But it's not accessible in the moment. And the friction of opening a browser feels too high to justify, standing there with a cart.

<Callout type="insight">
This is an education problem disguised as a lookup problem. The goal isn't just to answer "is this ripe?" — it's to build the user's intuition over time so they stop needing to ask. An app that makes itself unnecessary is a better app.
</Callout>

## Research

I interviewed 12 people about their produce-shopping behavior — ranging from regular home cooks to people who rarely cook. I wasn't looking for feature requests. I was looking for the moment the problem becomes real.

<Metrics>
  <Metric value="12" label="Users interviewed" sublabel="Mix of regular and occasional cooks" />
  <Metric value="9 / 12" label="Bought wrong produce" sublabel="Underripe, overripe, or wrong variety" />
  <Metric value="0" label="Opened phone in-store" sublabel="Browser friction was too high" />
</Metrics>

**Key findings:**

- Everyone relied on vague heuristics ("it smells good," "it feels firm") with no confidence in their own rules
- None had consulted their phone in the produce aisle — but would use a fast, focused app
- The real anxiety wasn't picking wrong produce — it was not knowing why it was wrong

## Design Approach

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Browse" title="Catalog — find any produce fast">
    Searchable, scrollable catalog organized by category. Each item shows the top-level selection rule — one sentence — before you even tap.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Quiz" title="3-question flow for a specific recommendation">
    What are you making? When will you cook it? How do you prefer it? Three questions, answered in under 20 seconds, produce a specific ripeness recommendation.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Learn" title="Visual guides for the actual selection moment">
    Illustrated guides for each produce item: what to look for, feel, smell, and avoid. Illustrations, not photos — for reasons explained below.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Remember" title="Feedback loop builds intuition over time">
    After cooking, a 'Good pick?' prompt reinforces what the user learned. Over time, the app becomes less necessary. That's the goal.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

## Design Decisions

### Illustrations over photography

Photo-realistic images create unattainable expectations — "mine doesn't look exactly like that." Hand-drawn illustrations communicate the principle (shape, color, texture) without the distraction of a perfect specimen.

This was the single most debated decision in the project. Every reference portfolio I found used photography. I believe it's wrong for this use case.

<ArtifactImage
  src="/case-studies/root/illustration-comparison.png"
  alt="Side-by-side: photo vs illustration for ripeness guide"
  caption="The same ripeness information shown as a photo (left) and illustration (right). The illustration communicates faster and causes less comparison anxiety."
  aspect="video"
/>

### Speed above everything

The core flow — arrive, answer 3 questions, get an answer — targets under 30 seconds. Users are standing in a store. A flow that takes 2 minutes is a flow that gets abandoned before the first tap.

Every additional question was cut. Every screen with optional fields was made optional, not removed.

### Single gamification mechanic

A streak for following up after cooking. Nothing else. No badges, no points, no leaderboards.

The reward is the knowledge itself — when the app stops being needed for a given vegetable, that's the win state. Gamification that competes with that goal is the wrong design.

## Outcome

Root is a speculative project. The design demonstrates:

- **Consumer mobile UX** — a 3-step quiz flow, catalog browsing with search and filter, illustrated educational content, feedback loops for long-term engagement
- **Speed-first design** — every flow targets the lowest-patience user, not the ideal engaged user
- **Illustration direction** — a designed visual language for produce that communicates faster than photography
