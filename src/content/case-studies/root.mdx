## The Problem

Most people have no idea how to choose produce. Is this avocado ripe enough for tonight? Which potato is right for roasting? How do you tell a good melon from a bland one?

The knowledge exists — farmers, chefs, and grandmothers know it — but it's not accessible in the moment when you're standing in the produce aisle, holding an avocado.

<Callout type="insight">
The insight that shaped this project: this is an education problem disguised as an app problem. The goal is not just to answer "is this ripe?" — it's to build the user's intuition over time so they stop needing to ask.
</Callout>

## Research

I interviewed 12 people about their produce-shopping behavior. Key findings:

- 9 out of 12 said they've bought produce that turned out to be wrong (underripe, overripe, or wrong variety for their recipe)
- All of them relied on vague mental heuristics ("it smells good" or "it feels firm") with no confidence in those rules
- None had ever consulted their phone in the produce aisle — the friction of opening a browser felt too high

The opportunity: a focused, fast, delightful experience that answers the question before you pick up the produce.

## Design Approach

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Explore" title="Browse by produce">
    A searchable catalog organized by type. Tap a vegetable or fruit to learn how to select it.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Quiz" title="Tell me what you need">
    A 3-question flow: What are you making? When will you cook it? How do you like it? The app recommends a specific ripeness and variety.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Learn" title="Visual guides and cues">
    Illustrated guides for each produce type: what to look for, what to feel, what to smell, what to avoid.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Remember" title="Build your intuition">
    A "Good Pick!" feedback moment after you cook reinforces learning. Over time, the app becomes less necessary — which is the goal.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

## Key Design Decisions

**Illustrations over photography.** Photo-realistic images create unattainable expectations ("mine doesn't look exactly like that"). Hand-drawn illustrations communicate the principle without the distraction of a perfect specimen.

**Speed above all.** The core flow — tap, answer 3 questions, get an answer — must complete in under 30 seconds. Users are standing in a store. Every extra tap is abandoned.

**Gamification lightly.** A streak for checking back after cooking is the only gamification mechanic. No badges, no points. The reward is the knowledge itself.

## Visual Direction

Leaf green, warm yellow, clay, and cream — the palette of a well-lit farmers' market stand. Rounded, friendly shapes. Plenty of white space so the illustrations breathe.

The app should feel like opening a favorite cookbook, not launching a productivity tool.

## Outcome

This is a speculative project. The design demonstrates mobile UX for consumer lifestyle apps: onboarding, a three-step quiz flow, a catalog browsing experience with search and filter, illustrated educational content, and a feedback loop for long-term engagement.
