## Context

Mayul Studio is a premium stationery brand I founded in Tel Aviv in 2020. It grew from a small local operation into an internationally shipping brand sold in stores across the city and online.

This case study documents the visual identity I designed from scratch — the reasoning behind every decision, and how the system has scaled across five years and dozens of products.

<Callout type="insight">
A stationery brand has a unique constraint: the product *is* design. Every customer physically handles the brand. The identity can't be decoration applied on top — it has to be design at the same level as the product itself.
</Callout>

## The Challenge

The premium stationery market is crowded with two opposing aesthetics: maximalist illustration-heavy brands (Rifle Paper Co., Papier) and cold minimalist white-label production brands. Neither felt like what I was making.

The challenge was finding a third position — quiet, considered luxury with warmth and a local character — and expressing it in a visual language specific enough to own, simple enough to execute consistently across embossing, offset print, and a phone screen.

## Process

<ProcessTimeline>
  <Step phase="Audit" title="Competitive landscape">
    Mapped 40+ premium stationery brands globally and locally. Identified the gap: warmth and precision together. Most brands chose one or the other.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Strategy" title="Positioning and brand character">
    Defined three brand attributes: considered, local, enduring. Not trendy. Not loud. Designed to age well — like the paper it prints on.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Identity" title="Wordmark and mark system">
    Developed a primary wordmark and a standalone secondary mark. Both needed to work at 5mm emboss depth and at 1200px banner width simultaneously.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="System" title="Full guidelines and rollout">
    Documented the complete system: color values, type scale, spacing, use rules, and examples across every application. The guidelines were designed to be used by print vendors, not just other designers.
  </Step>
</ProcessTimeline>

## Typography

Typography is the primary communication tool in a stationery brand — it appears on every surface and has to carry tone without illustration support.

<SplitView
  src="/case-studies/mayul-studio/typography-specimen.png"
  alt="Typography specimen showing primary and secondary typefaces"
  caption="Export: Figma frame 'Type Specimen' → /public/case-studies/mayul-studio/typography-specimen.png"
>

**Primary — Editorial serif.** Used for product names, campaign headlines, and anywhere the brand speaks with voice. Set with tight tracking and generous leading.

**Secondary — Geometric sans.** Used for product codes, labels, pricing, and UI. The contrast between the two faces creates hierarchy without needing size or color changes.

The rule: serif leads, sans supports. Never reversed.

</SplitView>

## Outcome

Mayul Studio has been shipping internationally for five years. The brand identity has never needed a refresh — it grew with the product line rather than constraining it.

The measure of a brand system isn't how it looks in a case study — it's whether it holds in the hands of a manufacturer who's never met you, or on a platform you didn't design for. Mayul Studio's system has passed both tests.

**What I learned:** A brand identity isn't a visual style — it's a set of decisions that travel independently. The system has to be opinionated enough to be recognizable and loose enough not to break.
