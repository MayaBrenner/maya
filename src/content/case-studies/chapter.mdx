## The Problem

Moving is not just a logistical event. It's a liminal state — the period between who you were and who you're becoming. You're leaving a version of your life and the objects that filled it, while simultaneously needing to build a new one somewhere unfamiliar.

The problem is that both sides of that equation are urgent and simultaneous. The person clearing out their apartment needs their dining table gone before their flight on March 15. The person landing in that same city next week needs a dining table before their family arrives. They need each other — but existing marketplaces treat them as strangers in a transaction, not two people at complementary moments of the same journey.

Facebook Marketplace is fast but lawless. Craigslist feels unsafe. eBay doesn't know you're local. None of them understand that the seller's story — *why* they're selling, *when* they need it gone — is actually the most useful information a buyer can have.

<Callout type="insight">
The gap isn't inventory. There are plenty of second-hand items available in every city. The gap is context, trust, and timing. Home Again is designed to bring all three into the same moment.
</Callout>

## Research

I interviewed 12 people who had relocated in the past 18 months — some moving out of a city, some moving in, some doing both at once. I focused on how they handled the furniture and objects problem, not just which apps they used.

Three distinct user types emerged clearly from the interviews:

**The Leavers** — need to clear everything before their key date. Urgency is the dominant emotion. They're not trying to maximize revenue; they're trying to minimize what they carry. Every item still in the apartment a week before they leave is a source of anxiety.

**The Arrivers** — moving in, unfamiliar with local options, need to furnish fast. They're price-sensitive but trust-sensitive more. They'd rather pay a bit more to someone whose story they can read than gamble on an anonymous listing.

**The Betweens** — doing both simultaneously: selling in one city while buying in another. The most stressed of all three types. They're managing two timelines at once and need reliable status updates from both markets.

## Design Questions

The research pointed to three core reframes that shaped the design:

**How might we** make the seller's context — their story and timeline — as visible as the item's price?

**How might we** give buyers real-time clarity on where a listing stands, without ambiguity or anxiety?

**How might we** lower the commitment threshold enough that someone can make real progress before they have to make a decision?

## Design Principles

Three rules governed every design decision:

**1. Seller context before price.** Every listing leads with who is selling and why. Lynne moving to Berlin on March 28 tells you more useful information than ₪1,450. Context is the product.

**2. Status as a first-class feature.** Every listing has a living state. Buyers should never have to wonder whether something is still available, or feel embarrassed asking. The status system makes that information ambient and unambiguous.

**3. Low-commitment first.** The default action is to ask a question, not to buy. Reserve and Pay is the last button, not the first. The funnel is: Ask → Meet → Reserve → Pay — and each step is discrete and reversible.

## The Status System

The most important design decision in Home Again is the status system. Every listing exists in one of four states:

**Available** — listed, active, all actions open. The default state. Pill: lime on pale yellow-green.

**On Hold** — someone has reserved it and paid a deposit. The seller is committed to them for 48 hours. Other buyers can join a waitlist and see exactly where they are in queue. Pill: orange on warm amber.

**Sold** — the item has a new home. The listing is archived. No ambiguity about whether to message. Pill: black on stone-gray.

**Back on Market** — the hold lapsed, the deal fell through, or the buyer backed out. The item is available again. Pill: blue on pale blue.

"Back on Market" is the most important state. In every other marketplace, a lapsed deal is invisible — the listing just silently reactivates and buyers never know whether it's a fresh listing or one that's already failed once. Home Again surfaces that history without shame. The message is: *"The deal fell through — it's available again."* That's information a buyer deserves to have.

## Seller Pages

Home Again's most differentiating feature is the seller page — a profile built around the seller's journey, not their inventory. When you tap into a seller's profile, you see their full context: where they're leaving, where they're going, their move date, and a short bio in their own words.

This isn't decoration. A buyer who knows that Lynne is leaving Tel Aviv for Berlin on March 28 — and has 4 items left to sell — can coordinate a pickup, combine items in one trip, and negotiate a bundle. They're not transacting with a stranger; they're helping someone close a chapter.

## Bundle Deals

The bundle feature is a direct response to research insight #3: movers don't want to coordinate 5 separate pickups with 5 separate buyers. They want one handoff, one conversation, one price.

Home Again lets sellers group their entire apartment into a single bundle — one price, one scheduled pickup, savings passed to the buyer. Lynne's whole-apartment bundle (sofa, lamp, dining table, chairs, rug) is ₪4,200 vs. ₪4,580 separately. The buyer saves ₪380; the seller saves three separate meetups.

  </Step>
  <Step phase="Read" title="The listing — seller context front and center">

  </Step>
  <Step phase="Ask" title="Per-listing chat — specific, contextual conversation">
    Chat is scoped to a single listing. Quick reply chips surface the three questions buyers always ask. The seller's status and price are pinned at the top so nothing gets lost in conversation.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Reserve" title="Deposit + escrow — low stakes to commit">
    A 20% deposit holds the item for 48 hours. The deposit is held in escrow until the handoff. Full refund if the meetup doesn't happen.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Meet" title="Meetup proposal — seller confirms, area revealed">
    Buyer proposes 3 time slots. Seller picks one. The area (e.g., Florentin, Tel Aviv) is shown before confirmation; the exact address is only shared after the seller confirms.
  </Step>
  <Step phase="Handoff" title="Release funds — item changes hands">
    Both parties confirm the handoff in-app. Escrow releases the full payment to the seller. The listing moves to Sold.
  </Step>

## Design Decisions

### Why not just show the address on the listing?

Because the handoff is what creates trust, not the address. Showing the exact address publicly invites bad actors and removes the natural safety mechanism of mutual confirmation. Home Again uses a staged reveal: general area visible on the listing, exact address shared only after both parties confirm a meetup. The seller is in control.

### Why seller pages instead of just listings?

Listings are items. Seller pages are people. A person leaving Tel Aviv for Berlin on March 28 is one of the most useful pieces of information a buyer can have — it tells them the urgency, the timeline, and the context behind every item that person is selling. The seller page turns that information into something scannable and actionable.

### Why a bundle feature?

Because moving is a wholesale problem, not a retail one. Most movers aren't trying to optimize each item's price — they're trying to minimize the number of strangers they have to invite into their apartment. The bundle feature collapses 5 separate transactions into 1 scheduled pickup, and prices it attractively to make the tradeoff obvious for buyers.

### Why 3 stacked action buttons instead of tabs?

Tabs imply equal weight. The three actions — Ask, Meet, Reserve — are a progression, not alternatives. Stacking them in order of commitment communicates that naturally. Asking is free. Meeting requires coordinating. Reserving requires money. The visual hierarchy says that before you read the labels.

The typography pairs Lora (a warm humanist serif) for display moments — headlines, item names, the wordmark — with DM Sans (clean, neutral, highly legible) for everything functional. The contrast reinforces the product's character: human and contextual in the moments that matter, clear and efficient in the moments that need to be quick.

Status colors are distinct and additive: lime for available (positive, present-tense), orange for on-hold (warm urgency), black for sold (neutral closure), blue for back-on-market (honest, informational). Each state carries its own emotional register.

## Takeaways

Home Again demonstrates:

- **Marketplace trust design** — building trust through context and commitment mechanisms rather than ratings or reviews alone. Seller stories replace anonymous profiles; escrow replaces faith.
- **Status system design** — treating status as a first-class feature rather than metadata. The "Back on Market" state is the product's most opinionated decision, and its most honest.
- **Seller-centric pages** — designing around people in motion rather than items in a catalogue. The seller page is the product's core differentiator.
- **Bundle UX** — turning a practical mover's constraint (I can't do 5 separate pickups) into a buyer incentive (one trip, one price, meaningful savings).
- **Funnel design for reluctant transactors** — people selling their furniture are not professional sellers. The low-commitment-first funnel respects both sides.
